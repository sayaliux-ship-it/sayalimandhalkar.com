Hey Nouri

Nine colors, one typeface, two shadows, and a shape language built entirely out of curves. This is the system as it actually ships on heynouri.com — measured, not estimated — plus the layer an app will have to add.

The carrot rule. Four elements, ~1.5% of the colored area. Carrot is a scarcity signal, not a brand color you decorate with. One carrot element per screen. Text on carrot is deep green at 5.35:1 — white on carrot is 2.97:1 and fails AA at every size. The site never does it.

03

Type

One family carries the entire brand. Manrope, variable, axis 200–800, self-hosted as six woff2 subsets. No display face, no serif, no rendered mono. It is a good fit for a specific reason: near-circular bowls read friendly and child-legible, while the tall x-height and tight apertures read dense and credible. That is the medical/warm split, in a typeface.

Four rules the scale encodes

  • Four line-heights, total. 1.10 display · 1.20 section headline · 1.40 titles and small text · 1.50 body. Memorize them.
  • Tracking tightens as size grows, and is never positive. −0.04em at 64px, −0.03em from 18–44px, −0.01em below 14px, 0 at body.
  • Sentence case everywhere. Zero text-transform: uppercase on the homepage — even the badges. There is no letterspaced-caps eyebrow in this system. Don't add one.
  • H1 is heavier than H2. 700 vs 600. The hero shouts; every section headline speaks.
  • Label line-height 0.90 is single-line only. It's tuned for pill badges that never wrap. If a label can wrap in the app, set 1.30 or the lines collide.

Pill is the dominant shape by a factor of four — badges, chips, tag rows, 240px circular team portraits. Note the inversion: buttons are the sharpest thing on the page at 8px while everything decorative is fully round. That keeps the CTA reading as a control rather than a sticker.

The curve motif

The mission line is "bend the obesity curve down" — and the curve is a literal shape motif running through the asset set: paired ellipse-up-xl / ellipse-down-xl arcs as section transitions, a curve framing the doctor portrait, photos arranged on an oval path, 90px circle bubbles, pill radius on 149 elements. No sharp diagonals, no triangles, no hard geometry anywhere. The dividers on this page are the same device.

06

Photography & voice

72 images on the homepage, and what is absent from them is the strongest statement in the whole system.

✓ Photograph
Real kids and teens, 9–18. Candid, active, outdoors, natural light. Tennis, running, forest, holding an apple. Never an adult alone; never a parent without a child.
✕ Never photograph
No before/after. No scales. No measuring tape. No food framed as the enemy. No sad-kid stock. No stethoscope-on-white clinical stock.
✓ Treatment
Hero: full-bleed cover, square corners, bottom scrim. Cards: cover at 24px radius. Cutouts: transparent PNG subjects on flat color grounds, contain. Avatars: 88px at 16px radius; care team at 240px fully circular.

Voice

"Your child gets someone who knows them, checks in, and has their back: on good days and tough ones."

  • "Your child" — not "your patient," not "the user." Consistently, everywhere.
  • Name the fear, then remove it. "Many of the families we work with feel like they've already tried everything."
  • Never diet language. No calories, no restriction, no "cheat," no "guilt-free," no "burn." The FAQ says so outright.
  • Numbers are specific and honestly hedged. "3–6 lb per month," "96% of families," "most families pay $0" — with an asterisk.
  • Gamification is described as fun, not compliance. "a game they actually want to play." Never "adherence," never "tracking your progress."
  • There is a script for the parent. The FAQ literally hands them words to say to their kid. That is the tone benchmark: practical, warm, non-clinical.
Every word on the site is written to the parent. The app is used by the child. The child-facing voice does not exist yet — it is the biggest single open question in this system.

✓ Carry forward
  • Pill-first shape language — progress rings, not bars; arc charts, not columns. On-brand and on-mission.
  • Alternating band composition → a card-stack feed with alternating grounds.
  • Two shadows only.
  • Sentence case, no tracked caps.
  • Opacity for hierarchy, not extra greys.
✕ Leave behind
  • The 200px section rhythm — correct on marketing, absurd on a phone. Use 24–48px.
  • Scroll-triggered reveal choreography — reads premium on a landing page, reads laggy in a daily app.
  • scale(1.2) on hover. There is no hover on touch; use scale(0.97) as a press state.
  • The 40px button.
  • outline: none — the CSS strips focus rings in two places. Build on the one good example instead: 2px solid warm-sun, offset 3px.
  • Parent-only voice in child-facing surfaces.
08

Open before app design starts

Five things the website cannot tell us, in the order they will block work.

Two apps, or one app with two modes?
The site sells to the parent and describes a daily app for the child. Parent check-ins, meal photos, coach chat and prescription management are different jobs with different privacy boundaries — a 16-year-old's coach chat probably should not sit in the parent's view. This decision reshapes the whole IA.
Is there already an app?
No App Store or Play links anywhere on the site, and the product UI is shown as flat PNG mockups (todays-win.png, weekly-plan.png, handphone.png). Confirm before treating this as greenfield — and pull those files either way; they are the closest thing to an existing product spec.
What is the child-facing voice?
Everything written so far is addressed to the parent. The daily user is nine to eighteen.
Where is the mark?
The wordmark is an inline SVG at a 285×75 viewBox. There is no icon or square lockup in the system — and an app needs one before anything else can be laid out.
9 to 18 is an enormous range.
A nine-year-old and a seventeen-year-old need different type sizes, different reward language, and different amounts of parent visibility. The web system has one register. The app will need at least two.
